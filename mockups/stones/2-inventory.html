Every stone says three things

Its shape says what kind of idea it is. Its material says which subject it belongs to. Its finish says how well you have held on to it. Six shapes, twelve materials and four finishes make 288 named stones, and no two are drawn exactly alike.

Four finishes, and a stone never goes back

A stone due for review is not dulled. It keeps the finish it has and is marked ready to polish. Reviewing it moves it one finish up.

The same kind of stone, never the same stone

Eight different writing principles. All are Amber Prisms, and each has its own tilt, tint and inclusions, fixed for good by the idea it stands for.

The rare one: a geode for a finished mission

Finishing a mission opens a geode. Each band is one stone from that mission in that stone's material, so the geode is a record of what was learned, and no two missions give the same one.

Optional: a milestone track

Not confirmed. Accept it or drop it; nothing else on these boards depends on it. Milestones mark totals, not ideas, so they are deliberately not cut stones: no shape, material or finish to read. There are three families, each a different kind of object, and you can tell them from concept stones at a glance.

Spires, for focused hours

A clear crystal cluster on rock. Each threshold adds spires, so a bigger cluster means more hours. Named by the threshold.

Rings, for people you brought in

Rings carved from one piece of deep red stone. One ring, three linked, then a chain of ten.

Meteorites, for limited editions

A dated piece of etched iron with a gold edge. Each is tied to one occasion and cannot be earned afterwards. The last one here shows how a milestone looks before it is earned.

Naming: “[threshold] Spire”, “Ring of [number]”, “[occasion] Meteorite”. Eight fixed milestones plus editions, against 288 concept stones, which keeps them rare.

How an idea gets its stone

The same idea must always be the same stone, on every device, for ever. So the stone is decided once, written down, and never worked out again.

stone = {
  cut:      concept.kind,       // one of 6, stored
  material: concept.subject,    // one of 12, stored
  seed:     hash(concept.id),   // stable number
  finish:   fromReviews(concept) // cut → polished → brilliant → star
}

Four small values per idea. No image and no SVG are stored; the engine draws from these.

Should embeddings choose the stone? I tested it. No.

The idea was to place each concept in embedding space so that related concepts get related materials. I embedded 55 hand-labelled concept titles with Voyage (voyage-3.5) and assigned each to its nearest subject and nearest kind.

CheckResult
Right subject34 of 55 (62%)
Right kind of idea27 of 55 (49%)
Decided by a hair22 of 55 had the top two subjects within 0.02 of each other
Same idea, rewordedKept its material 5 times out of 8

Typical misses: “Borrow checker” landed in design and craft, “Occam's razor” in maths, “Spaced repetition” in writing. The deeper problem is the third and fourth rows: a small change of wording, or a new embedding model, moves a stone to a different material, and a stone must never change.

The simpler rule wins. The model that pulls the idea out of the learner's material already reads the surrounding text, so it labels kind and subject there, the labels are stored, and the learner can correct one if it is wrong. Embeddings could still be used later to suggest neighbouring stones, but not to decide what a stone looks like.

Limits of the test: titles only, one-line descriptions as anchors, one run. Fuller text would score higher, but would not fix the instability. The Voyage key is also limited to three requests a minute until billing is added.
